# Indian Law on Medical Records: Research Brief

**Purpose:** Verify whether the ANSWER_RUN medico-legal claim — that Indian
hospitals face real legal exposure from poor medical record-keeping — is
grounded in statute and case law, or is speculative.

**Verdict: The claim is real.** Multiple statutes, regulations, and Supreme
Court judgments create concrete legal obligations around medical records.
Hospitals that fail to maintain, retain, or produce records face adverse
inferences, negligence findings, and consumer court liability. The gap
between law-on-paper and ground reality is itself a risk vector.

---

## 1. Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010

- **Scope:** Requires registration of all clinical establishments (hospitals,
  nursing homes, clinics, dispensaries, sanatoriums) — public and private,
  including AYUSH. Exempts military facilities only.
- **Standards obligation:** Section 12 requires compliance with minimum
  standards prescribed by the National Council, which include standards for
  record maintenance. The Central Government prescribes these via rules.
- **Penalties:** Running unregistered: up to Rs 50,000. Withholding
  information or giving false information: up to Rs 5,00,000. Corporate
  officers personally liable.
- **Adoption problem:** The Act applies directly only to Arunachal Pradesh,
  Himachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Sikkim (passed resolutions pre-enactment).
  Bihar, Rajasthan, UP, Uttarakhand, Jharkhand, Assam, Haryana adopted
  later. Major states (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, West
  Bengal) have NOT adopted it — they rely on their own state-level acts.
- **Relevance to patient-360:** Where adopted, this Act mandates minimum
  record-keeping standards. Where not adopted, the regulatory gap is even
  wider, making internal standards (NABH) and professional regulations
  (IMC/NMC) the only enforceable baseline.

## 2. IMC (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002

The Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics)
Regulations, 2002 — now administered by the National Medical Commission
(NMC) post-2019 — contain the primary statutory obligation on individual
doctors to maintain medical records.

### Key provisions (Regulation 1.3):

- **1.3.1:** "Every physician shall maintain the medical records pertaining
  to his/her indoor patients for a period of 3 years from the date of
  commencement of treatment in a standard proforma..."
- **1.3.2:** "...and shall be ready to provide them to the patient/authorized
  attendant on request." If the patient or authorized person requests
  records, the physician must furnish them.
- **Appendix 3** of the Regulations prescribes the standard proforma for
  indoor case records.

### Analysis:

- The **3-year minimum** is a floor, not a ceiling. Consumer courts and the
  Supreme Court have consistently held that destroying records after 3 years
  does not shield hospitals from adverse inferences if litigation is pending
  or foreseeable.
- The regulation applies to **individual physicians**, not institutions. But
  hospitals are vicariously liable for their employed/retained physicians
  under both contract law and CPA.
- **Maharaja Agrasen Hospital v. Master Rishabh Sharma (2019) SC:**
  Cited Regulation 1.3 directly. The Supreme Court (Justice Indu Malhotra)
  held that failure to maintain medical records of a baby in compliance with
  Regulation 1.3.2 constituted evidence of negligence. Citation: (2019)
  AIRONLINE SC. Cited by 28 subsequent decisions.
- Post-NMC Act 2019: The NMC inherited these regulations. The Registered
  Medical Practitioner (Professional Conduct) Regulations under NMC continue
  the record maintenance obligations.

### Gap:

- 3 years is absurdly short for pediatric cases (limitation period doesn't
  start until the child turns 18), obstetric cases (cerebral palsy claims
  surface years later), and chronic disease management.
- No regulation specifies digital record standards, audit trails, or
  tamper-proofing requirements for electronic records.

## 3. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

- **Medical services are "services" under CPA.** This was settled under the
  1986 Act by *Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995) 6 SCC 651*
  and carried forward unchanged into CPA 2019.
- **Deficiency in service** (Section 2(11)): Includes any fault,
  imperfection, shortcoming, or inadequacy in the quality, nature, and manner
  of performance of a service. Poor documentation = potential deficiency.
- **Jurisdiction:**
  - District Commission: up to Rs 1 crore
  - State Commission: Rs 1 crore to Rs 10 crore
  - National Commission (NCDRC): above Rs 10 crore
- **Limitation:** 2 years from the date of cause of action (Section 69).
  But courts have been liberal in computing "date of knowledge" for medical
  negligence.
- **Burden of proof:** The complainant must establish deficiency. However,
  when records are in the exclusive possession of the hospital and the
  hospital fails to produce them, the burden effectively shifts via adverse
  inference (see Section 5 below).
- **Product liability (new in 2019):** Chapter VI introduces product
  liability for defective goods/services. Medical devices and
  pharmaceuticals used during treatment could trigger this.

## 4. Adverse Inference Doctrine for Missing Medical Records

This is the single most powerful legal weapon against hospitals with poor
record-keeping.

### Principle:

Under Indian evidence law (now BSA 2023, Section 37; formerly Indian
Evidence Act, Section 114 illustration (g)): **"The court may presume that
evidence which could be and is not produced would, if produced, be
unfavourable to the person who withholds it."**

### Application in medical negligence:

Indian courts have consistently drawn adverse inferences against hospitals
that fail to produce medical records. The reasoning is straightforward:

1. Medical records are created by and in the custody of the hospital.
2. The patient has no independent means of proving what treatment was given.
3. If the hospital withholds or "loses" records, the court presumes the
   records would have shown negligence.

### Key cases on adverse inference:

- **Girdhari Lal Maternity Hospital v. Smt. Nirmala (2008):** Delhi District
  Court drew adverse inference for non-production of medical records that
  were in the hospital's possession. The hospital's failure to produce was
  treated as admission of the negligence alleged.
- **P.R. Padmanaban v. Govindammal (1992):** Madras HC held that
  non-production of medical records leads to adverse inference against the
  treating physician/hospital.
- **Rajani Kumari P.V. v. Poyyanil Hospital (2024):** Kerala State
  Commission drew adverse inference where original records were not produced
  and only copies (with wrong entries) were furnished.
- **C.R. Gautam v. Indian Spinal Injuries Centre (2023):** NCDRC held that
  hospital deliberately not supplying case papers warranted adverse inference
  for "sheer negligence."
- **Mrsanita Singh v. GNCTD (2016):** CIC held hospital cannot deny medical
  records when facing negligence allegation; adverse inference drawn.

### Practical effect:

A hospital that cannot produce complete, contemporaneous records for a
patient who files a negligence complaint is **presumed negligent** unless it
can rebut the presumption with other evidence. This is not academic — it is
how cases are actually decided in consumer courts daily.

## 5. Key Supreme Court Cases

### 5.1 V. Kishan Rao v. Nikhil Super Speciality Hospital (2010) 5 SCC 513

- **Bench:** Justices Asok Kumar Ganguly and G.S. Singhvi
- **Holding:** Overturned *Martin F. D'Souza* on the requirement of
  mandatory expert evidence in medical negligence. Held that in some cases,
  the principle of *res ipsa loquitur* ("the thing speaks for itself")
  applies — the negligence is so obvious from the facts that expert testimony
  is not required.
- **Record-keeping relevance:** When a hospital's own records show deviation
  from standard protocols, the records themselves are the evidence. No expert
  needed to interpret them.
- **Impact:** Cited by 350+ subsequent decisions. This is the current
  governing law, superseding the stricter requirements of *D'Souza*.

### 5.2 Samira Kohli v. Dr. Prabha Manchanda (2008) 2 SCC 1

- **Subject:** Informed consent.
- **Holding:** Established comprehensive principles on informed consent in
  India. A doctor must disclose: (a) nature and procedure of treatment,
  (b) risks involved, (c) alternatives available, (d) consequences of
  refusing treatment. Consent must be specific to the procedure performed.
- **Record-keeping relevance:** Informed consent must be **documented**.
  A signed generic consent form does not constitute informed consent.
  The hospital's records must show what was specifically explained and
  what the patient specifically agreed to.
- **Impact:** Cited extensively in NCDRC and state commissions. Every
  negligence case now examines the consent documentation.

### 5.3 Martin F. D'Souza v. Mohd. Ishfaq (2009) 3 SCC 1 (AIR 2009 SC 2049)

- **Bench:** Justice Markandey Katju
- **Holding:** Laid down elaborate guidelines for medical negligence cases.
  Required that before a doctor is issued notice in a negligence case, the
  complaint must be referred to a competent medical board. Applied the
  **Bolam test** (a doctor is not negligent if they act in accordance with a
  practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion).
- **Record-keeping relevance:** Paragraph 106+ deals extensively with
  hospital duties. The judgment required hospitals to maintain and produce
  records.
- **Status:** Partially overruled by *V. Kishan Rao* on the mandatory
  expert-committee referral point. The Bolam test application remains valid.
  Cited by 502 subsequent decisions — still frequently referenced even where
  *Kishan Rao* governs.

### Summary of SC position:

The current law (*Kishan Rao* + *Samira Kohli*) means:
1. Hospitals must maintain records showing what was done and why.
2. Informed consent must be specifically documented, not generic.
3. Where records show deviation from protocol or are missing, *res ipsa
   loquitur* can apply — no expert evidence needed.
4. Missing records trigger adverse inference.

## 6. NABH 6th Edition (2025) — Information Management System (IMS)

The National Accreditation Board for Hospitals & Healthcare Organizations
(NABH) is a constituent board of the Quality Council of India. NABH
accreditation is voluntary (except where state governments mandate it), but
it sets the de facto standard for hospital quality in India.

### IMS Chapter — Key Requirements:

- **IMS.1:** The hospital shall have a documented policy and procedure for
  medical records management covering creation, review, distribution,
  maintenance, retrieval, and disposal.
- **IMS.2:** Medical records shall contain sufficient information to identify
  the patient, support the diagnosis, justify the treatment, document the
  course and results, and promote continuity of care.
- **IMS.3:** Every medical record entry shall be dated, timed, and
  authenticated by the person making the entry.
- **IMS.4:** Medical records shall be reviewed regularly for completeness,
  accuracy, and timeliness.
- **IMS.5:** The hospital shall define retention periods for medical records
  (NABH recommends longer than the IMC 3-year minimum — typically 5-10 years
  for adult inpatient records, and until majority + 3 years for pediatric).
- **IMS.6:** Access controls, confidentiality, and security of medical
  records. Patient's right to access their own records.
- **IMS.7:** For electronic health records — audit trails, backup,
  disaster recovery, data integrity validation.
- **Measurable elements:** Each IMS standard has specific measurable elements
  that assessors score during accreditation surveys. Failure to meet IMS
  standards can result in denial or withdrawal of accreditation.

### 6th Edition (2025) changes:

- Stronger emphasis on digital records and interoperability.
- Explicit requirements for audit trails in EHR systems.
- Alignment with Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) standards.
- Patient safety event reporting linked to medical records.

### Legal significance:

- NABH standards are increasingly referenced by courts as the **benchmark
  of reasonable care**. A hospital that is NABH-accredited but fails to meet
  IMS standards faces internal compliance risk AND legal exposure.
- A hospital that is NOT NABH-accredited is held to at least the IMC/NMC
  minimum standards, but courts often reference NABH standards as the
  reasonable standard of care regardless.

## 7. Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA), 2023 — EHRs as Evidence

The BSA replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 effective 1 July 2024. It
modernizes the treatment of electronic records as evidence.

### Key provisions:

- **Section 61:** "Contents of documents may be proved either by primary or
  by secondary evidence." Electronic records are documents under the Act.
- **Section 63:** Admissibility of electronic records. This is the critical
  section (replaces old Section 65B of the Evidence Act).
  - **63(1):** Any information in an electronic record, whether stored,
    recorded, or copied in optical or magnetic media, produced by a computer
    is deemed a document and is admissible.
  - **63(2):** Conditions: The computer must have been in regular use, data
    must have been fed in the regular course of activities, the computer
    must have been operating properly.
  - **63(4):** **Certificate requirement.** A certificate signed by a person
    occupying a responsible official position must accompany the electronic
    record, identifying the electronic record and describing the manner of
    its production. This is mandatory for admissibility.

### Relevance to EHR systems:

1. **An EHR printout is not automatically admissible.** It must be
   accompanied by a Section 63(4) certificate from a responsible officer
   of the hospital identifying the system, confirming regular operation,
   and authenticating the output.
2. **Audit trails matter.** Courts will examine whether the EHR system was
   operating properly (Section 63(2)(b)) and whether data was fed in the
   ordinary course (Section 63(2)(a)). An EHR without audit trails cannot
   satisfy these conditions.
3. **Tampering risk:** If the opposing party challenges the electronic record,
   the hospital must demonstrate system integrity. No audit trail = no
   reliable demonstration of integrity.
4. **Already being applied:** As of 2025-2026, High Courts (Kerala, Madras,
   Punjab-Haryana, Delhi, Madhya Pradesh, Jharkhand) are actively requiring
   Section 63(4) certificates for electronic evidence. The Madras HC in
   *Assistant Commissioner v. Vetrivel Minerals (2025)* engaged in detailed
   analysis of Section 63 requirements.

### Practical implication for patient-360:

Any EHR system used by an Indian hospital must be designed so that:
- A Section 63(4) certificate can be generated for any record.
- Audit trails prove the system was operating properly and data was entered
  in the ordinary course.
- Records cannot be retroactively modified without the modification being
  logged and visible.

## 8. Practical Reality of Record-Keeping in Indian Hospitals

### The gap between law and practice:

- **Tier 1 (large corporate chains — Apollo, Fortis, Max, Manipal, Narayana
  Health):** Generally have EHR systems (often vendor solutions like Cerner,
  Epic, or Indian products like Practo, BahmniEMR). Compliance with IMC/NMC
  and NABH standards is reasonably good. But even here, completeness and
  timeliness of documentation is physician-dependent.
- **Tier 2 (mid-size hospitals, 50-200 beds):** Mixed. Many have partial
  digitization — lab results and billing are digital, but clinical notes are
  still paper-based. Records are often incomplete. OPD records are rarely
  maintained beyond the patient's personal file (which they may or may not
  carry).
- **Tier 3 (small nursing homes, clinics, single-doctor practices):** Minimal
  to no record-keeping. Paper registers with one-line entries. OPD patients
  have no records at all except a prescription slip. This is the vast
  majority of Indian healthcare delivery.
- **Government hospitals:** Large volumes, understaffing, paper-based systems.
  Records are frequently lost, illegible, or incomplete. The RTI Act 2005
  has increased demand for records, but supply-side capability has not kept
  pace.

### Specific failure modes:

1. **No unique patient identifier:** Without Ayushman Bharat Health Account
   (ABHA) integration, patients visiting multiple facilities have fragmented
   records. The same patient may have different MRNs at different hospitals.
2. **Paper records lost in transit:** Between departments (radiology to ward,
   ICU to general ward), records are misplaced.
3. **Incomplete nursing notes:** The most common documentation gap. Nursing
   observations, medication administration records, and vitals charts are
   frequently incomplete or illegible.
4. **Consent forms:** Generic, pre-printed consent forms with no
   procedure-specific information. Violates *Samira Kohli* requirements.
5. **Discharge summaries:** Often prepared by junior residents, not reviewed
   by the treating consultant. May omit complications, adverse events, or
   deviations from the treatment plan.
6. **OPD records:** In most Indian hospitals, OPD records stay with the
   patient. If the patient loses the file, there is no hospital copy. This
   is a massive medico-legal gap.
7. **Retroactive entries:** In paper systems, entries can be added or modified
   after the fact with no audit trail. In poorly designed EHR systems, the
   same problem exists digitally.

### Why this matters for medico-legal exposure:

Every one of the failure modes above maps directly to a legal risk:

| Failure Mode | Legal Risk |
|---|---|
| No unique ID | Cannot prove continuity of care |
| Lost records | Adverse inference — presumed negligent |
| Incomplete nursing notes | Cannot prove monitoring was done |
| Generic consent | Violates *Samira Kohli* — battery claim |
| Poor discharge summary | Cannot prove what was communicated |
| No OPD records retained | Cannot defend against negligence in OPD |
| Retroactive modification | Tampering allegation — criminal liability |

## Conclusion: Is the Medico-Legal Claim Real?

**Yes, unambiguously.**

The legal framework creates a cascade of liability:

1. **Statute** (CEA 2010, CPA 2019) mandates standards and creates consumer
   court jurisdiction over medical services.
2. **Regulations** (IMC 2002 / NMC) require individual physicians to maintain
   records for at least 3 years.
3. **Case law** (V. Kishan Rao, Samira Kohli, Martin D'Souza, Maharaja
   Agrasen Hospital) establishes that missing or incomplete records trigger
   adverse inference, res ipsa loquitur, and shift the effective burden of
   proof to the hospital.
4. **Evidence law** (BSA 2023, Section 63) requires EHR systems to meet
   specific technical standards (audit trails, certificates) for records to
   be admissible.
5. **Accreditation** (NABH 6th Ed) sets the practical standard of care that
   courts reference.

The practical reality — where most Indian hospitals fall far short of these
requirements — is not a defense. It is the risk surface. A patient-360
system that enforces completeness, maintains audit trails, and generates
Section 63-compliant output directly addresses the largest single source of
medico-legal liability for Indian hospitals: the inability to produce
complete, trustworthy medical records when challenged.
